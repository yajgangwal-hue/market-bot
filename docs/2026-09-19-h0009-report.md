before any result existed. Adjudicated the same day.*

**Decision: REJECTED.** All three thresholds fail clauses A, B and C.

Spearman(threshold, return delta) = **−1.0000**. Every loosening is
worse, and worse in order.

## The eight questions, answered

**1. Does RSI 35–40 contain useful candidates?**
**At the name level yes, at the portfolio level no.** The breadth pass
measured higher forward excess vs SPY for (35,40] than for ≤35. Traded
through the actual portfolio — with its stops, sizing, holding cap and
slot constraint — the same names realise **less**: mean P&L $14 against
$73 for ≤35 at rsi36, and negative at rsi37 and rsi38. The forward-
excess measurement did not survive contact with the execution machinery.

**2. Do they improve the actual constrained portfolio?**
**No.** −14.34, −54.51 and −55.27 points of total return. Drawdown
deepens at every step. Sharpe falls 0.5107 → 0.3920 → 0.0887 → 0.0840.

**3. How much comes from empty slots versus displacement?**
This is the central finding and it is not what the hypothesis assumed.
At rsi36, EMPTY-slot additions contributed **+$3,023 across 202 trades
— about $15 each**, and swaps contributed **+$863**. Both are positive
and both are trivially small. The loss comes from somewhere else
entirely: **churn**. Of the baseline's 698 trades only **425 survive**
at rsi36, **273 at rsi37** and **201 at rsi38**. Loosening the gate does
not add trades at the margin — **it reshuffles the whole portfolio**,
and 71% of the baseline's book is gone by rsi38.

**4. Does it survive adjacent thresholds?**
**No — the opposite.** The gradient is perfectly monotone downward,
Spearman = −1.0000, with no positive delta anywhere. This is not a
spike that might be real at one point; it is a consistent decline.

**5. Does it survive time/regime robustness?**
Not reached — clause E is evaluated only for configurations passing
A–D, and none did. Year-by-year is reported below and is negative in
most years at every threshold.

**6. Does it improve excess return after costs?**
**No.** Excess vs SPY moves from −224.55 points to −238.89, −279.07 and
−279.83. Costs rise slightly ($13,868 → $14,295 → $13,845 → $14,499)
but costs are not the story; selection is.

**7. Is the evidence strong enough to justify another experiment?**
**No.** Three sweeps of this parameter now agree. This closes the
entry-threshold direction.

**8. Is there any defensible promotion candidate?**
**None.** Not one configuration passed a single clause combination.

## Slot competition and displacement — the central analysis

Each configuration's entries classified against what the **baseline**
did on the same session.

| | A rsi36 | B rsi37 | C rsi38 |
|---|---|---|---|
| total trades | 774 | 870 | 939 |
| newly eligible (RSI 35–38) | 237 | 464 | 611 |
| **KEPT** (baseline took it too) | **425** / $36,547 | **273** / $5,499 | **201** / $7,306 |
| **EMPTY** (baseline traded nothing) | 202 / **+$3,023** | 361 / **−$22,677** | 469 / −$7,712 |
| EXTRA (added alongside) | 55 / +$191 | 83 / −$4,301 | 82 / −$7,232 |
| **SWAPS** | 92 / **+$863** | 153 / +$9,244 | 187 / **−$8,684** |
| swap win rate | 57.6% | 53.6% | 52.9% |

**The hypothesis assumed additions would mostly fill idle slots. They
do — and it doesn't help.** At rsi36 the 202 empty-slot additions
earned **$15 each**. The damage is in the 273 baseline trades that
simply stopped happening.

This is the mechanism the experiment was built to find, and it is not
"new candidates displace old ones one-for-one." It is that **admitting
more candidates re-orders the entire selection**, because the
correlation-bucket cap and the 3-per-day cap are resolved in candidate
order. A 35–38 name arriving in a bucket locks out the ≤35 name that
would have claimed it later. By rsi38 only **21%** of the baseline's
book survives.

Worst single swaps, named: rsi36 took QCOM (−$1,338) instead of VST
(+$811) on 2018-10-19; rsi38 took AMAT (+$59) instead of CIEN (+$3,097)
on 2019-05-09.

| A rsi36 | ≤35 | 537 | **$73** | +0.036 | 50.7% | 36.1% | 5.52% | −3.99% |
| A rsi36 | **35–38** | 237 | **$14** | −0.015 | 49.4% | 32.5% | 4.95% | −4.04% |
| B rsi37 | ≤35 | 406 | $26 | −0.105 | 47.0% | 39.7% | 5.50% | −4.09% |
| B rsi37 | **35–38** | 464 | **−$16** | −0.073 | 48.7% | 37.1% | 4.95% | −4.25% |
| C rsi38 | ≤35 | 328 | $12 | −0.144 | 46.6% | 39.9% | 5.46% | −4.17% |
| C rsi38 | **35–38** | 611 | **−$2** | −0.024 | 49.4% | 35.2% | 5.00% | −4.15% |

Two things here, and the second is the more important.

**The newly admitted band is worse in every configuration** — $14 vs
$73, −$16 vs $26, −$2 vs $12. The MFE column explains why: 35–38 names
run **4.95–5.00%** in favour at their best against ≤35's **5.46–5.52%**,
while risking the same (MAE ≈ −4.0% to −4.2%). A shallower dislocation
gives the 2.5-ATR stop and the 20-bar cap the same risk with less
upside. That interaction is invisible to a forward-return measurement,
which is why the breadth pass saw a hump that does not exist in P&L.

**And the ≤35 band itself degrades as the gate loosens** — $73 → $26 →
$12. The surviving ≤35 trades are a *different, worse subset*, because
the best ones were locked out of their buckets by 35–38 names arriving
first. Loosening the gate damages the candidates it was supposed to
leave alone.

## What this closes

Three independent sweeps of `rsi_entry` now agree — a two-year sweep,
the 2026-09-07 decade sweep, and H-0009. Loosening the entry gate
raises exposure and drawdown and lowers return, monotonically, whether
measured in 5-point steps or 1-point steps. **The entry-threshold
direction is closed.**

It also closes something larger: the RSI 35–40 hump found by the
breadth pass was the last surviving lead from that work, and it does
not survive the capacity constraint. The name-level and portfolio-level
measurements disagree, and the portfolio one is the one that pays.
